# Part I — Executive Summary

## 1. Business Challenge

PLG’s primary IAM challenge is not the absence of control language. It is the absence of a consistent operating model for turning access-control requirements into timely, accountable, and measurable work.

Existing practices depend heavily on:

- Email
- Manual handoffs
- Manager knowledge
- System-specific procedures
- Informal exceptions
- Scattered evidence

These conditions increase the possibility that users could receive excessive access, retain permissions after changing roles, or remain active after leaving PLG.

---

## 2. Project Objective

The project created a fictional target-state IAM operating model that connects:

> Business requirement → Risk → Framework requirement → Control objective → Workflow → Owner → Evidence → Test → Metric → Exception → Improvement

The project demonstrates how governance requirements can become operational processes that:

- Employees can follow
- Managers can own
- IT can execute
- Security can monitor
- GRC can assess
- Leadership can evaluate

---

## 3. Material Risks

The most significant risks include:

1. Departed personnel retaining access
2. Users receiving excessive permissions
3. Transferred personnel retaining prior-role access
4. Privileged access lacking enhanced oversight
5. Contractor and courier access remaining active
6. Segregation-of-duties conflicts
7. Incomplete access reviews
8. Weak exception governance
9. Inadequate control evidence
10. Unresolved control deficiencies

The fictional risk assessment identified delayed termination access as the highest-priority concern because it combines a likely operational failure with potentially severe impact.

---

## 4. Target Operating Model

The proposed model establishes seven primary workflows:

| Workflow | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Joiner-Mover-Leaver | Align access with the workforce lifecycle |
| Access Request and Approval | Validate need and authorization |
| Provisioning and Verification | Implement approved access accurately |
| Periodic Access Review | Confirm that access remains necessary |
| Privileged Access | Apply enhanced controls to elevated permissions |
| Termination and Emergency Revocation | Remove access rapidly and completely |
| Exception and Risk Acceptance | Govern temporary deviations |

Each workflow defines:

- Trigger
- Inputs
- Roles
- Decisions
- Service levels
- Evidence
- Failure conditions
- Escalation
- Metrics

---

## 5. Governance and Accountability

The design assigns accountability across:

- Executive leadership
- HR
- Department Managers
- System Owners
- IT
- Security
- GRC
- Facilities
- Vendor management
- Contractors and sponsors

The model separates:

- Request
- Approval
- Provisioning
- Verification
- Assessment

This separation reduces the likelihood that one person can initiate, approve, implement, and validate the same high-risk access.

## 6. Evidence and Assessment

The project establishes an evidence catalog covering:

- Workforce events
- Access requests
- Approvals
- Provisioning
- MFA
- Segregation-of-duties reviews
- Termination records
- Access reviews
- Privileged-activity reviews

Assessment procedures use examine, interview, and test concepts to evaluate whether controls are appropriately designed and capable of producing reliable evidence.

---

## 7. Measurement and Monitoring

The proposed KPI and KRI model measures:

- Request completion
- Approval completeness
- Termination timeliness
- Access-review completion
- Overdue revocations
- Expired exceptions
- Privileged-access approval
- Evidence completeness
- Control-test exceptions
- POA&M completion

Metrics are assigned targets, warning thresholds, Critical thresholds, owners, reporting cadences, and required responses.

---

## 8. Implementation Priorities

PLG should prioritize:

1. Establishing an authoritative HR-to-IT workforce-event process
2. Implementing termination and emergency-revocation controls
3. Inventorying privileged and contractor accounts
4. Creating role and entitlement catalogs
5. Standardizing requests and approvals
6. Establishing periodic access reviews
7. Formalizing exceptions
8. Creating the evidence repository
9. Introducing control testing and POA&M tracking
10. Automating selected handoffs and reconciliation

---

## 9. Expected Business Value

The target model would help PLG:

- Reduce unauthorized access
- Improve termination speed
- Limit privilege accumulation
- Strengthen contractor governance
- Improve individual accountability
- Produce more reliable evidence
- Detect control failures sooner
- Clarify decision authority
- Reduce repeated manual confusion
- Support risk-based improvement

---

## 10. Executive Decisions Required

Leadership would need to:

- Approve IAM ownership.
- Approve risk and exception authority.
- Support HR, IT, Security, and GRC coordination.
- Fund priority remediation.
- Require System Owner participation.
- Establish acceptable service levels.
- Approve the use of monitoring and escalation thresholds.
- Decide which residual risks may be accepted.

---

## 11. Executive Conclusion

PLG’s access risk cannot be addressed through policy language alone.

Effective IAM requires:

- Clear ownership
- Defined workflows
- Reliable handoffs
- Risk-based decisions
- Timely execution
- Verifiable evidence
- Meaningful metrics
- Formal escalation
- Continuous improvement

The proposed design provides a practical foundation for converting access-control requirements into sustainable business operations.

## 13. Connection to My Operations Background

Time-sensitive operations depend on:

- Clear roles
- Accurate information

- Service levels
- Exception handling
- Documentation

These same principles apply to GRC engineering.

A control cannot succeed simply because it is well written. It must function inside the real organization where people work under deadlines, systems have limitations, responsibilities overlap, and exceptions occur.

---

## 14. Key Lesson

The strongest lesson from this project is:

> A control is not operational merely because it appears in a framework or policy.

A usable control must answer:

- Who starts the work?
- What information is required?
- Who approves it?
- Who performs it?
- What can go wrong?
- What evidence proves completion?
- What happens when the deadline is missed?
- Who accepts the remaining risk?
- How is the process improved?

---

## 15. Human-Centered GRC Engineering

Security processes must account for how people actually work.

If a workflow is confusing, inaccessible, excessively slow, or disconnected from operational reality, people may bypass it. Those workarounds can become control failures.

Human-centered GRC engineering means:

- Using clear language
- Reducing unnecessary friction
- Providing sufficient context
- Assigning realistic responsibilities
- Designing usable escalation paths
- Preserving human judgment
- Making secure behavior easier to execute
- Measuring whether the control works in practice

---

Policies establish expectations. Technology enforces some decisions. People perform and review the work. Evidence shows what occurred. Metrics reveal whether the process remains healthy. Leadership decides how much residual risk the organization will accept.

My operations experience is not separate from my cybersecurity path. It provides a practical foundation for understanding:

- Process
- People
- Standards
- Safety
- Documentation
- Exceptions
- Escalation
- Accountability
- Real-world consequences

That perspective is the foundation of the type of GRC practitioner I am becoming: one who can connect requirements to the way work actually happens.
